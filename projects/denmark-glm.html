08 / Warwick coursework / Generalised linear models

A better rate model puts diabetes-linked deaths in context.

Grouped Danish data showed overdispersion under a Poisson model. After switching to a negative-binomial GLM and predicting a no-diabetes counterfactual, the estimate was 74,063 deaths, or 6.45% of the observed total.

By Faris Erhan · 17 October 2026 · 7 minute read

University of Warwick coursework · R · grouped mortality data

Tools

My role
Fit, diagnose and compare models; calculate a counterfactual

The coursework PDF could not be located for this build. AIC totals are explicitly marked for source-file verification.

A death count needs a denominator. These Danish data cover people at risk over time, so the model estimates rates by dividing expected deaths by person-years. I compared a Poisson model with a negative-binomial alternative, then asked how the fitted total changed if the diabetes indicator were set to “no” for every group.

The data are grouped across diabetes status, sex, 20 five-year age bands and four five-year periods. Each row records deaths and person-years at risk. The 320 grouped cells contain 1,147,700 observed deaths over 1996–2015.

The central lesson was diagnostic: a familiar count model can be too restrictive when the data vary more than its assumptions allow. The model change affects the counterfactual, so I make that path visible instead of presenting the final percentage alone.

The Poisson model missed the spread

Scroll through the model sequence. The figure is a process diagram; the diagnostic marks are schematic because the source coursework plots are not available here.

Diagnostics led from Poisson to negative binomial

Model choice / Scroll to follow the repair

DATA GRAIN

One row is a grouped person-time cell.

Each cell combines diabetes status, sex, an age band and a five-year period. Person-years at risk enter as an offset, so the model works on rates rather than raw counts alone.

AGE SHAPE

Age bands fit better than one straight age slope.

Treating age group as a factor reduced deviance from 16,506 to 5,562, using 18 additional parameters. The likelihood-ratio test was below 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶.

FIRST FIT

Poisson residuals curved and spread out.

The residual plots showed a curved pattern and larger spread at higher fitted values. That is a warning that the Poisson variance assumption did not describe the data well.

MODEL REPAIR

Negative binomial allows extra variation.

I refit with a negative-binomial GLM. Its variance can exceed its mean; the scale-location plot was flatter afterwards. This supported the model choice, while leaving uncertainty and omitted factors to discuss.

COUNTERFACTUAL

Set diabetes to no and compare predictions.

I compared the model’s observed-setting prediction with a second prediction setting diabetes to “no”. The difference is an estimate under this model, not proof that diabetes caused each death.

Model selection has one unresolved number

The write-up says the age-group-by-diabetes interaction improved AIC. The notes contain two conflicting pairs: verify against coursework: 4,193 → 3,814 (preferred in the talking points) and reconcile: 4,190 → 3,810. The coursework PDF was not located, so this page does not use either pair as a settled result.

A separate total check put the fitted overall count at 1,147,387 against 1,147,700 observed, a gap of 313. The coursework notes attribute this small difference to the negative-binomial model’s non-canonical log link.

The final counterfactual still needs cautious wording. “Attributable under the model” describes a fitted comparison. It does not establish what would have happened to each person, and other factors such as obesity or income could explain some of the difference.

“The estimate is attributable under the model, not proof of causation.”

Interpretation limit · Denmark GLM analysis

What this misses

  • The no-diabetes estimate depends on the fitted model and its included covariates.
  • Grouped data do not describe individual histories or establish individual causation.
  • Potential confounders, including obesity and income, may explain part of the association.
  • The coursework PDF and raw dataset were unavailable for this portfolio build; AIC totals remain unresolved.

What I’d do next

Recover the coursework and source data, verify the AIC comparison, reproduce the plots and recalculate the counterfactual from the fitted model.

Then compare age-group factors with smooth age splines, checking fit and interpretability on the same grouped data.

Test sensitivity to plausible omitted risk factors if suitable data can be obtained.

Why this analysis matters

The useful result is a defensible chain: choose the right denominator, test model assumptions, change the model when diagnostics warrant it, and label the final counterfactual as an estimate. A numerical result is only as persuasive as the path that produced it.
